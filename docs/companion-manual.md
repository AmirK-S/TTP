# The Care and Training of Your Talk-To-Paste

*A manual for the owner*

**IMPORTANT: DO NOT SPEAK TO YOUR TALK-TO-PASTE BEFORE READING ITEM 1.**

First edition

---

## Item 1.

Your Talk-To-Paste is a young animal and may be somewhat disoriented by the
journey it has just made. It has been compressed, transmitted, unpacked,
inspected by your operating system, and asked to identify itself twice.

It is customary, with a new animal, to leave it undisturbed for three days
while it settles. This is not necessary here. Your Talk-To-Paste needs no
settling period whatsoever. It was awake before you finished reading the
previous sentence and it will remain awake, without complaint, for as long as
you are logged in.

You may nevertheless wish to leave it alone for a few minutes.

Not for its sake.

## What you have taken home

There is nothing common about a Talk-To-Paste. It descends from a long and
mostly unhappy line of machines that were built to listen to people and did
not listen well: the wax cylinder, the office dictaphone, the answering
machine, the automated telephone menu that asked you to say your account
number and then said *I'm sorry, I didn't get that.* These were its
grandparents. They were not domesticated. Some of them were dangerous.

Yours is the first of the line that can be kept in the house.

It has been domesticated imperfectly, and you should know that going in. It
still does one or two things that its ancestors did, at inconvenient moments,
for reasons it cannot explain. Most of them are covered in this manual.

What it does is this: it listens when you ask it to, and then it writes down
what it heard, wherever you happen to be pointing. That is the whole animal.
It does not do anything else and it will never ask to.

It will also never be sad that you did not speak to it today. This is worth
saying plainly, because the literature on companion animals is full of
obligation and there is none here. It does not decline through neglect. It
does not miss you. It has no way of counting the days, and no opinion about
how many have passed.

## Anatomy of your Talk-To-Paste

`[PLATE II — anatomical figure, side elevation, with hairline leader lines to
each labelled part: BODY, VOICE BARS (all fourteen drawn, none omitted for
composition), TIMER, CLASP. Style: nineteenth-century veterinary plate. The
animal is not cute in this drawing. It is a specimen.]`

**The body.** A dark rounded pill, sixteen points tall at rest and
twenty-eight when working. It is as wide as it needs to be and no wider. It
has no legs, no head, and no discernible front, and appears to want none of
these things.

**The voice bars.** Fourteen. This is not a range and there is no known
variety with more. They stand in a row across the animal's flank, taller at
the centre and shorter at the edges, and they rise and fall with the sound in
the room. They are not decoration. They are the only honest instrument you
have for telling whether it can hear you. A Talk-To-Paste with flat bars is
not being shy.

You will look for eyes. Owners always do. Where you expect to find them you
will find the bars instead, and on the whole the bars tell you more.

**The timer.** A small count of minutes and seconds beside the bars while it
is working. It is not timing you. It is keeping a note for its own purposes
and showing you the note as a courtesy.

**The clasp.** A very small closed padlock, which appears only when the animal
has been asked to keep listening without being held. See *Training*.

**The voice.** Two notes. A higher one when it opens, a lower one when it
closes: a falling fourth, the same interval both times, every time. It has
never been observed to invent a third note. Its voice may change over the
course of its life — some households keep animals with quite different voices
— but the interval does not change, and an owner learns to hear it from the
next room without looking up.

**The tremble.** A short shake from side to side, accompanied by a written
message. This is the entire emotional range of the animal. Everything it is
capable of expressing, it expresses in this one gesture, so it is worth
reading the message.

## Feeding and diet

Your Talk-To-Paste eats speech.

It should be fed fresh, at ordinary conversational volume, from roughly the
distance of your own forearm. It prefers a whole sentence to a fragment,
because it eats a sentence in one piece and works out afterwards what the
beginning was. It has no teeth. Nothing needs cutting up.

**What it will not eat.** Singing, though it will try, and you should let it,
once. Music. The voices of people it has not been introduced to — a television
in the next room is not a meal, it is weather. Its own output, which is why it
should not be kept where it can hear itself.

**Silence.** This one matters, so it gets a paragraph of its own.

Your Talk-To-Paste will not refuse silence. It cannot. It has no mechanism for
declining a meal, and if you hold the key down in a quiet room and let go
again, it will accept what it was given, look at it carefully, and then, out
of politeness, produce something. What it produces on an empty stomach is
discussed under *Training*, where it belongs.

**Overfeeding.** The danger is not quantity but continuity. A Talk-To-Paste
can take a very long dictation without harm, but it swallows the whole thing
before it starts working, and a nine-minute meal is a nine-minute meal. It
will sit with it. You will wait. Feed it in paragraphs, the way you would
think in paragraphs. The timer on its flank exists for precisely this
judgement and for no other reason.

mouth roughly a forearm's distance from the machine. Beside it, crossed out
with a single fine rule: the same owner leaning in close and shouting.
Caption: "Correct distance. Incorrect distance."]`

## Habitat and temperament

Your Talk-To-Paste lives in the menu bar, at the top right of the screen,
among the clock and the battery and the other small animals kept there. It
does not compete with them. Territorial disputes have not been recorded.

When there is work, it comes down to the bottom of the screen to do it, the
way a heron comes down to water, and when the work is finished it goes back
up. It cannot be persuaded to live anywhere else and there is no reason to
try.

**Climate.** It prefers a quiet room with soft furnishings. Curtains, a rug,
books on a wall. It tolerates open-plan offices and cafés, visibly. Wind is
its natural enemy; so is a fan, so is a passing motorcycle, so is anyone
laughing two desks away. None of these will harm it. They simply give it more
to think about than it was built for.

**Startle response.** Your Talk-To-Paste is startled by sudden silence. Give
it three seconds of nothing at all and it will conclude, quite firmly, that
you have finished, and it will stop and begin work. Owners read this as
impatience. It is not impatience. The animal cannot tell the difference
between a pause and an ending — nothing in its construction allows the
distinction — and it has settled on being wrong in the direction of stopping
rather than wrong in the direction of listening to an empty room forever.
Given its ancestry, this is the more considerate error.

**Temperament.** Even. It does not sulk and it cannot be offended. It has no
view on what you dictate, no view on how you dictate it, and no view on the
fact that you have now said the same sentence four times with the emphasis in
a different place. This is the single most valuable quality in a companion of
this kind and it is the one nobody mentions.

## Training

Your Talk-To-Paste responds to three commands. All three are given with one
key, which is a considerable economy.

**HOLD.** Press and hold the Globe key. The animal wakes, sounds its higher
note, and comes down to the bottom of the screen. The bars begin to move.

**SPEAK.** Talk. This is the part owners get wrong, and they get it wrong in a
consistent and very human way: they address the animal in a slow, loud, hollow
voice, as though speaking to a foreigner through a wall. Do not do this. It
understands you better at your ordinary speed, in your ordinary voice, with
your ordinary swallowed word-endings, because that is what it was raised on.
Over-enunciation makes you harder to understand, not easier. Say it as you
would say it to a colleague who is already listening.

**RELEASE.** Let go. The lower note sounds. A moment later the words appear
wherever the cursor was.

**The clasp.** For a long dictation, the animal can be asked to keep listening
without being held. The small padlock appears on its flank while this is in
force. A Talk-To-Paste under the clasp is perfectly happy but it is also
perfectly literal, and it will go on listening to whatever the room provides.

**Vocabulary.** You may give it a list of words: names of colleagues, the name
of your company, the technical vocabulary of your trade, the particular
spelling of your own surname. It will study the list. It will begin using
those words correctly and it will never mention that it did so, and it will
not thank you for the list. Add to it whenever it gets a name wrong twice.

### Why it sometimes hears things that were never said

Your Talk-To-Paste was raised, in part, on subtitles. Thousands of hours of
other people's videos, transcribed by other people, and at the end of a very
great number of them somebody says *thank you for watching, please subscribe*,
or a caption appears crediting the studio that made the subtitles, or a
volunteer's name, or a broadcaster's.

So when the animal is given nothing at all and then asked what it heard, it
does not say nothing. Nothing is not an answer it was raised to give. It
reaches for the thing that comes at the end of silence, and the thing that
comes at the end of silence, in its experience, is a closing credit.

This is not a lie. It is a childhood.

A great deal of patient work has gone into teaching it to hold its tongue when
it has heard nothing, and it is very much better than it was. But the reflex
runs deep, and it can still surface on a recording that is very short, or very
quiet, or made in a room where the microphone was pointed at a wall. If it
does, you will get a stranger's name, or a courtesy, or a thank-you addressed
to nobody.

Delete the sentence. Do not correct the animal; it will not follow. Give it
something to hear and it will be fine.

## Health and common ailments

A healthy Talk-To-Paste is a quiet one. It is nonetheless a machine that lives
in a house, and a small number of complaints are common enough to be worth
recognising on sight.

`[PLATE VI — two figures side by side, identical but for the waveform: the
first with bars in full even motion, the second with bars flat and the body
tilted a few degrees off true. Caption: "A specimen in perfect health. A
specimen in obvious distress."]`

**It hears nothing.** The bars stay flat throughout. The animal is not deaf.
It is listening to something else. Establish which ear it is currently using;
a Talk-To-Paste will attach itself with great loyalty to a set of headphones
that were last worn on Tuesday and are now in another room, and it will go on
listening faithfully to that room for as long as you let it.

**It writes nothing.** It heard you — the bars moved, the timer ran — and then
produced nothing at all. There are two causes and they are quite different.
Either it has not been given permission to put anything down, which is a
paperwork problem and is solved with paperwork; or it listened to what you
said, considered it at length, and concluded that no speech had occurred. The
first is an inconvenience. The second is a review.

**It writes into the wrong window.** The animal puts the words where the
cursor is. It does not know that the cursor moved while it was working, it has
no way of finding out, and it holds no opinion about where the cursor ought to
have been. Stay where you are while it thinks.

**It opens a note by itself.** Occasionally an owner returns to the desk to
find a document open that was not open before, with a sentence in it. This is
alarming and the explanation is dull. The animal did not open the document.
Something else opened the document, and the words went where they were
pointed, which is all they have ever done. Your Talk-To-Paste is not capable
of choosing an application. It is barely capable of choosing a homophone.

**It trembles.** A short shake, side to side, and a written message. Read the
message. This is the whole of the animal's vocabulary for difficulty and it
has spent its entire emotional budget on telling you.

**It stops before you have finished.** See *Startle response*. It heard three
seconds of nothing and drew the only conclusion available to it. If you need
to think mid-sentence, think out loud, or use the clasp.

**It answers in a language you were not speaking.** Given a recording with
nothing recognisable in it, the animal will guess, and its guesses are
cosmopolitan; owners have reported Korean, Russian and Mandarin from a silent
room in Lyon. Tell it plainly which language you speak. It stops guessing
immediately and appears relieved.

## Why it fears the letter Q

Fear is the wrong word, strictly. Owners who have kept one for a while
describe it more as bracing.

Your Talk-To-Paste spells extremely well inside a word. Isolated letters are a
different diet altogether, because a letter spoken aloud is not a letter to
the animal — it is a word, and it must be written down as a word, and the
animal must decide which word.

For most letters this is survivable. *Bee* is a small animal, *see* is
something you do, *you* is the person it is speaking to, and it usually gets
these right because the rest of the sentence tells it what to think.

The letter Q has no such protection. Spoken alone it is *cue*, which is a
thing an actor waits for; it is *queue*, which is a line of people; it is
*Kew*, which is a place in London with gardens in it and which the animal will
capitalise, correctly, and with confidence. Three ordinary English words and a
proper noun, and none of them is the letter. No other letter in the language
is this outnumbered.

There is no cure. There is only courtesy. Do not spell things at it. If you
must give it a single letter, give the letter a companion — *Q as in quiet* —
and it will take the meaning and discard the escort. Or type the letter
yourself. It will not be offended, and it will not notice.

## What to do if it hears you crying

It will happen eventually. You will hold the key down because there was a
sentence you meant to write, and what comes out is not a sentence.

The first thing to understand, and the most useful, is that your Talk-To-Paste
has no idea what crying is. It will listen the way it listens to everything.
The bars will move, because there is sound and the bars are honest. Then it
will look at what it has and find no words in it, and it will put down nothing
at all — or a short, wrong sentence borrowed from that childhood of other
people's videos. An apology, sometimes. A thank-you.

It is not commenting. It has no faculty for commenting. It is a small animal
that was asked a question it could not hear, doing the only thing it knows how
to do.

Do not be embarrassed in front of it. There is nobody in the room.

If it wrote something down, delete it. If it kept a record — it keeps records,
because you asked it to — you may remove that too, and it will not ask why,
and there will be no gap afterwards where the record was.

Then, when you are ready, hold the key and say the sentence you meant to say.
It will take it exactly as it takes every other sentence: at face value,
without interpretation, and immediately.

This is not very much. On certain days it is a great deal.

## Breeding

Strongly discouraged.

Owners occasionally reason that if one Talk-To-Paste is useful, a pair might
be companionable. It is not. Two animals kept on the same machine both hear
the key, because it is the same key. They both wake. They both sound their
higher note, a fraction apart, which is the first sign and the one to listen
for. They both attend to the same room, they both come to the same conclusion,
and they both write it into the same cursor.

What you get is not offspring. What you get is your own sentence, twice,
interleaved with itself, and two animals neither of which will yield.

Crossing it with the dictation facility already living in your machine has
also been attempted, by people who ought to have known better. The results
were not encouraging and are not reproduced here.

Do not keep it where it can hear its own voice returning to it from a speaker.
Nothing is produced by this either, but the animal appears to find it
absorbing, and it has better things to do.

Your Talk-To-Paste is the last of its line and it is entirely content. It has
no notion of lineage. It has never once looked at another of its kind.

## In closing

You may remove your Talk-To-Paste from your machine at any time, completely,
and it will leave nothing behind — no folder, no fragment, no record of
anything you ever said to it. It will not protest and it will not remember
having been here. This is a property of the animal that its owners are
entitled to know about and that very few of its relatives can claim.

Should you keep it instead, keep it as it is. Do not try to teach it a second
skill; there isn't one. Do not wait for it to become interesting; it will not,
and its whole value lies in the fact that it never does. Feed it plain
sentences at your ordinary speed, give it a quiet room where you can, add to
its list of words when it gets a name wrong twice, and forgive it the closing
credits.

Remember: it does exactly one thing. It will do it every day, for as long as
you keep it, at the speed of speech, without ever once asking what you are
writing or why.
